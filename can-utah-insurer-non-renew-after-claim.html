Can my insurer non-renew me after a claim in Utah?

What Utah's insurance code says about claims, inquiries, and non-renewal, in plain language.

By the CoverageCheckin editorial team. Published November 16, 2026. Sources checked November 16, 2026. See our editorial policy.

If you filed a claim and then received a non-renewal notice, or you are worried that calling your insurer about damage could cost you your policy, Utah law has specific rules. This page quotes and summarizes them in plain language. If you have already been non-renewed, our non-renewal page explains how we can refer you to a licensed Utah agent, and our non-renewal notice guide covers the general timeline.

The general rule: renewal with 30 days' notice

Under Utah Code 31A-21-303 (Subsection 4), a policyholder generally has a right to have a policy renewed, and that right ends if the insurer delivers or mails a notice of intention not to renew at least 30 days before the policy expires or completes an anniversary. The statute then carves out specific protections, below.

One wind, hail, or lightning claim

Subsection 5(b) says an insurer may not fail to renew a homeowners policy solely on the basis of a claim for damage that results solely from wind, hail, or lightning, is not preventable by the exercise of reasonable care, and is the only such claim within a 36-month period (Utah Code 31A-21-303(5)(b)). The protection is narrow: it covers one qualifying claim in 36 months, and the word "solely" matters. For more on storm claims, see hail and roof coverage in Utah.

A phone call or question is not a claim

Subsection 4(c) says an insurer may not fail to renew a policy because of a telephone call or other inquiry that references a policy coverage and does not result in the insured requesting payment of a claim (Utah Code 31A-21-303(4)(c)). A separate statute, Utah Code 31A-19a-212, says personal lines rates may not permit a premium increase, at issue or at renewal, because of a call or inquiry that does not result in a request for payment of a claim.

Your right to know why

Under Subsection 7, if a notice of cancellation or non-renewal does not state with reasonable precision the facts behind the decision, the insurer must send that information within 10 working days after receiving your written request, and the notice must tell you about your right to ask (Utah Code 31A-21-303(7)). The NAIC's consumer guide likewise says you may ask the insurer for the reason for a non-renewal (NAIC, A Consumer's Guide to Home Insurance).

What the law does not say

Beyond the protections above, we did not find a broader rule in Section 31A-21-303 that bars non-renewal after other kinds of claims. We are not lawyers, and we do not interpret how these rules apply to a particular claim. If you believe an insurer did not follow them, the Utah Insurance Department has a complaint process.

Your claims history report

The Utah Insurance Department describes C.L.U.E. (Comprehensive Loss Underwriting Exchange) as a claims history database that insurers use when underwriting or rating a policy (Utah Insurance Department, Information for Consumers). The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says LexisNexis C.L.U.E. reports up to seven years of home and personal property claims, and will provide one free report every 12 months on request (CFPB, LexisNexis C.L.U.E.). The CFPB's list also includes A-PLUS Property by Verisk, which reports home claims and loss history and offers one free report every 12 months on request (CFPB, 2025 Consumer Reporting Company List). Requesting your own report does not hurt your credit scores, according to the CFPB.

Questions to bring to your insurer or an agent

  • What exactly is the reason for the non-renewal, in writing?
  • Was the claim counted as wind, hail, or lightning damage, and was it the only one in 36 months?
  • Was a call I made recorded as a claim or as an inquiry?
  • What does my claims history report show?
