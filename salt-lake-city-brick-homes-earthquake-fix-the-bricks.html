Salt Lake City brick homes: the Magna earthquake, Fix the Bricks, and your policy

Facts from the state URM strategy, Salt Lake City, FEMA, and the Census Bureau, plus questions to ask an agent.

By the CoverageCheckin editorial team. Published October 28, 2026. Sources checked October 28, 2026. See our editorial policy.

Salt Lake City has a lot of older brick houses, and the 2020 Magna earthquake showed how unreinforced brick can fare in even a moderate quake. Earthquake is one of the perils the Utah Insurance Department lists as typically not covered by a homeowners policy, along with flood, earth movement, and mudslide (Utah Insurance Department, homeowners insurance). How old Salt Lake City's homes are

Census Bureau estimates (American Community Survey, 2020 to 2024) put the median year built for housing units in Salt Lake City at 1965. Of about 101,188 housing units, 25,698 (25.4%) were built in 1939 or earlier, and 54,756 (54.1%) were built before 1970 (ACS 2024 5-year, table B25034, via Census Reporter). We computed the percentages from those counts.

Age matters for brick. A state and FEMA strategy for unreinforced masonry (URM) buildings estimates there are more than 140,000 URM buildings across the Wasatch Front. It cites 1961 as one dividing line for earthquake-resistant construction in a federal study of Davis, Salt Lake, Utah, and Weber counties, says homes with URM exterior walls may have been built as late as 1966, and lists 1975 (masonry parapet and chimney reinforcing) and 1977 (the first statewide building code) as later benchmark years (Wasatch Front URM Risk Reduction Strategy, 2021). Year built alone does not tell you how a particular house was constructed.

What the Magna earthquake did

The magnitude 5.7 earthquake centered in Magna on March 18, 2020, led to a major presidential disaster declaration (DR-4548). According to the URM strategy:

  • The State Historic Preservation Office reported nearly 150 historic buildings damaged, including the Sears and Fisher mansions and the Rio Grande Depot, with damage ranging from major structural cracks to failed parapets and masonry walls and damaged or collapsed chimneys.
  • Total damage was estimated at roughly $100 million.
  • Residents described Salt Lake City's Liberty Wells neighborhood as hit particularly hard, with multiple URM buildings damaged.
  • A survey of historic buildings found that non-retrofitted URM buildings consistently showed more damage than retrofitted ones, and most of the retrofitted properties had gone through Salt Lake City's Fix the Bricks program.
  • The strategy also cautions that Magna's ground motions were lower than those expected in a larger Wasatch fault earthquake.

The source for all of the above is the Wasatch Front URM Risk Reduction Strategy, prepared for the Utah Division of Emergency Management and FEMA.

Fix the Bricks

Fix the Bricks is Salt Lake City's seismic retrofit program for brick homes. FEMA's case study says a 2016 pilot grant of over $700,000 retrofitted 29 homes, and that a 2019 grant of more than $5 million aims to retrofit about 200 more (FEMA case study). The city's current application page says:

  • The program serves low-income homeowners in Salt Lake City whose brick houses were built before 1975.
  • Applications are closed, and applicants on the existing list may wait 10 years or more.
  • The average retrofit cost is $20,000.
  • Documents requested from applicants include proof of insurance.

(Salt Lake City, Fix the Bricks application). The city describes the work as a seismic retrofit focused on items such as roof-to-wall connections and chimney bracing, and notes it is not a general masonry repair program (Salt Lake City, Fix your home).

Questions to bring to an agent

  • Does my policy include an earthquake endorsement, and what deductible applies to it?
  • Does my insurer ask whether the house is brick, and whether it has been retrofitted?
  • If a chimney or parapet falls in an earthquake, how would my policy treat that damage, with and without an earthquake endorsement?
  • Would a completed retrofit change anything on my policy or its price?
