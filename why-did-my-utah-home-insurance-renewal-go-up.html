Why did my Utah home insurance renewal go up?

What the Utah Insurance Department and the Legislature's staff say is driving premiums, plus your notice rights.

By the CoverageCheckin editorial team. Published November 9, 2026. Sources checked November 9, 2026. See our editorial policy.

A higher number on a renewal offer is one of the most common reasons Utah homeowners start looking at their policy. This page lays out the public reasons Utah regulators and legislative staff give for rising premiums, and the questions you can take to your insurer or a licensed agent. To see your own change in dollars and percent, try our premium change calculator, and to get a reminder ahead of next year's date, set up a free renewal check.

How much have Utah premiums gone up?

In a June 17, 2026 presentation to the Legislature's Business and Labor Interim Committee, legislative staff said Utah homeowners premiums have risen approximately 59 percent since 2021, to roughly $213 per month for the average Utah homeowner (Office of Legislative Research and General Counsel, Homeowners' Insurance Affordability, June 2026). That works out to about $2,556 a year (our arithmetic: $213 times 12).

The increases have not been even across the state. Using ZIP-code data the Insurance Commissioner's office collected from insurers, KUTV reported in September 2025 that average premiums from 2018 to 2022 rose 41% in 84404 (North Ogden, Harrisville, Farr West, Plain City), 46% in 84037 (Kaysville, Fruit Heights), 40.8% in 84015 (Clearfield, Sunset, Clinton, West Point) and in 84074 (Tooele), 40% in 84096 (Herriman), and 39.9% in 84043 (Lehi, Saratoga Springs) (KUTV, September 15, 2025).

The reasons the state gives

The Utah Insurance Department says insurers set premiums based on the expected cost of future claims, and that state law requires rates not be excessive, inadequate, or unfairly discriminatory. It lists these factors for an individual home (Utah Insurance Department, Why is my home insurance premium increasing?):

  • Where you live and your claims history.
  • The cost to replace your home and contents, which the Department notes is not the same as market value.
  • Your insurance score and your deductible.
  • Your home's age, and the age and condition of its construction materials and roof.
  • Fire protection: distance to a hydrant and the local fire department's quality, which set your fire protection class.

It also lists wider pressures: more frequent and severe weather events, higher labor and materials costs for construction and repairs, inflation and interest rates, the rising cost of litigation, and reinsurance (the insurance that insurance companies buy), which it says has risen significantly.

The legislative staff presentation adds two Utah specifics: wildfire is the state's largest environmental peril, with increased risk driving claim likelihood, and inflation in construction inputs since 2020 has raised claim costs (OLRGC, June 2026). Insurance Commissioner Jon Pike told KUTV that inflation, rising home values, higher construction costs, and more frequent natural disasters are the main drivers, and that Utah's rates were lower than neighboring states' for years and are catching up (KUTV). If wildfire may be a factor at your address, see Utah's high-risk WUI boundary.

Who reviews the increase?

According to the same KUTV report, Utah law does not let the Insurance Department set or approve rates; it can review them. That is different from states where a regulator must approve a rate before it is used.

What Utah law says about notice of a higher renewal

Under Utah Code 31A-21-303 (Subsection 6(a)), if an insurer offers to renew at higher rates or on less favorable terms, the new terms take effect on the renewal date only if notice was sent at least 30 days before the old policy expires. If not, they take effect 30 days after the notice, and you may cancel the renewal policy during that 30-day period. Subsection 6(b) lists exceptions, such as an increase that applies to the whole class of policies. Our 90, 60, and 30 day renewal checklist shows where that date falls.

Questions to bring to your insurer or an agent

  • Which part of my premium changed: the base rate, my dwelling limit, a surcharge, or a lost discount?
  • Did my dwelling (replacement cost) limit go up automatically, and how was the new figure estimated?
  • Did my home's rating details change, such as roof age, protection class, or wildfire area?
  • Did any claim or my insurance score affect this renewal?

Your declarations page lists your limits and deductibles, so comparing this year's with last year's is a simple way to spot what moved. The Department publishes yearly comparison tables with sample premiums from large Utah insurers, which show how much prices for the same sample home can differ. Before you share details with anyone selling a policy, you can verify that the agent is licensed in Utah.
