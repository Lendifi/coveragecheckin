Does homeowners insurance cover flood in Utah?

What the Utah Insurance Department and FEMA say about flood, mudflow, sewer backup, and basements.

By the CoverageCheckin editorial team. Published November 9, 2026. Sources checked November 9, 2026. See our editorial policy.

No. The Utah Insurance Department says standard homeowners and renters policies do not cover losses due to flooding, including post-wildfire mudflow, and that flood insurance must be bought as a separate policy (Utah Insurance Department, Flood Insurance FAQ, updated July 2026). Because a new flood policy usually has a waiting period, the months before your homeowners policy renews are a natural time to ask about it. Does flooding really happen in Utah?

The Department's FAQ calls Utah a desert state with low yearly rainfall, but says burn scars from wildfires significantly increase local flooding risk because charred soil cannot absorb water. The state's floodplain program adds severe thunderstorms and poor drainage as causes (Utah Division of Emergency Management, Flood Insurance). Snowmelt matters too: on April 18, 2023, Governor Cox declared a state of emergency for flooding and the avalanches, landslides, rockslides, and mudslides that came with that spring's heavy snowpack, and the state had deployed more than 1 million sandbags by April 7 (Office of the Governor, April 18, 2023). For local examples, see our pages on Utah County burn-scar debris flows and St. George flash floods.

Where flood insurance comes from

The Department says some private companies write flood insurance in Utah, but the vast majority of flood policies are federally backed through FEMA's National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP), and the agent who writes your homeowners policy may be able to write one. NFIP flood insurance is available only in communities that take part in the program. The state's floodplain program explains that federally backed flood insurance is made available in communities that agree to regulate development in their mapped floodplains (Utah Division of Emergency Management, NFIP). FEMA's Community Status Book (report dated November 9, 2026) lists 229 Utah communities in the program and 25 not in it, including Cedar Hills in Utah County, Farr West and Hooper in Weber County, and Hideout in Wasatch County (FEMA Community Status Book, Utah). You can look up your own city or county there.

The state's flood page lists NFIP limits of $250,000 for the building and $100,000 for contents. It also notes that Utah has no statute requiring sellers to make specific flood disclosures to buyers, that you can look up an address on FEMA's flood maps, and that a lender may require flood insurance whether or not the home is in a mapped flood zone (Utah Division of Emergency Management, Flood Insurance).

The 30-day wait

Under federal rule 44 CFR 61.11, a new NFIP policy or added coverage generally takes effect at 12:01 a.m. on the 30th day after the application and premium payment. The exceptions include:

  • Map changes: during the 13 months after a revised flood map takes effect, initial coverage starts the day after application and payment.
  • Post-wildfire flooding: for privately owned property affected by flooding on federal land caused or made worse by post-wildfire conditions, coverage starts the day after application and payment if it is bought no later than 60 days after the fire's containment date.
  • Loans: when flood insurance is first bought in connection with making, increasing, extending, or renewing a loan, coverage can start at the loan closing if it is applied for and paid at or before closing.

Words that matter: flood, mudflow, mudslide, sewer backup

  • Flood: under the NFIP, a general and temporary condition of normally dry land being covered by water or mudflow, affecting two or more acres or two or more properties, one of them yours.
  • Mudflow vs. mudslide: the NFIP covers mudflow (liquid mud carried by water). Mudslides and other earth movement are excluded by both standard homeowners policies and NFIP policies; the Department says they can be covered through a difference in conditions (DIC) policy.
  • Water from inside: a burst pipe or broken water heater is not a flood, though it may fall under a homeowners policy.
  • Sewer backup: covered by the NFIP only if an active surface flood in the area directly caused it.

All four definitions come from the Department's Flood Insurance FAQ.

Basements

If your home has a basement, note that NFIP basement coverage is limited. Per the Department's FAQ, the NFIP treats any area with a floor below ground level on all sides as a basement. Covered items there include furnaces, water heaters, central air, sump pumps, electrical boxes, foundation elements, stairways, unfinished drywall, and connected washers, dryers, and freezers. Finished drywall, paint, finished flooring, built-in cabinets, furniture, electronics, and clothing in a basement are not covered.

Questions to bring to an agent

  • Is my address in a mapped flood zone, and does my lender require flood insurance?
  • Would an NFIP or a private flood policy fit my home, and how do their basement and contents terms differ?
  • Does my homeowners policy offer a water or sewer backup endorsement, and what does it exclude?
  • If I want coverage before spring runoff or monsoon season, when would it start?

The state's flood page suggests confirming with the Utah Insurance Department that an agent and company are licensed to sell flood insurance before you sign or pay; you can verify a Utah agent's license online. Earthquake is a separate exclusion; see does homeowners insurance cover earthquakes in Utah?
