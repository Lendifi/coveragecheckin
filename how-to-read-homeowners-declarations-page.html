How to read your homeowners declarations page

A field-by-field guide to the summary page of your policy, and how to use it at renewal.

By the CoverageCheckin editorial team. Published October 19, 2026. Sources checked October 19, 2026. See our editorial policy.

The declarations page, often called the dec page, is the short summary at the front of your homeowners policy. It lists who and what is insured, the policy dates, your coverage amounts, your deductibles, and your premium. It is also where you can see what changed when a renewal arrives, and our free renewal check can remind you before that date so you have time to look.

This guide goes through a dec page one field at a time, using definitions published by the National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) and the Utah Insurance Department. If your renewal premium changed, our premium change calculator shows the difference in dollars and percent. For one coverage that is usually missing from a dec page, see whether homeowners insurance covers earthquakes in Utah.

Where the dec page fits in your policy

According to the NAIC, a homeowners policy typically has four parts:

  • Declarations (information) page. The policy number, effective dates, insured address, mortgage holder, coverages, coverage limits, premium, and discounts.
  • Insuring agreement. A summary of the coverages and the kinds of losses covered. It can be changed by endorsements, which are listed on the dec page.
  • Exclusions. Perils and property the policy does not cover.
  • Conditions. What you are expected to do after a loss, plus definitions of the terms used elsewhere in the policy.

The dec page tells you the amounts. The other parts tell you the rules. Reading one without the other can give an incomplete picture.

The fields, one at a time

Named insured and property address

The NAIC suggests confirming that the names and the insured address are correct, and notes that in most cases everyone on the mortgage is named as an insured. It also notes that an error in the address could cause problems with a claim (NAIC).

Policy period

These are the dates the policy is in force. The NAIC notes that a mortgage company checks that coverage continues without a break, and that if coverage lapses, the lender can buy insurance on the property and charge you for it (NAIC).

Property coverages (often labeled A through D)

Many insurers use letters for the main coverages. The Utah Insurance Department describes them this way in its 2024 comparison tables (page 6):

  • Dwelling (Coverage A): the house and structures attached to it.
  • Other Structures (Coverage B): fences, sheds, unattached garages, and other structures not attached to the house.
  • Personal Property (Coverage C): your belongings, such as furniture, electronics, appliances, and clothing.
  • Loss of Use (Coverage D): additional living expenses above your normal costs while the home is being repaired.

The department notes that the letters are a convention "used by many companies," so your insurer's labels may differ.

Liability coverages (often labeled E and F)

The same tables describe Personal Liability (Coverage E) as covering your financial loss if you are sued and found legally responsible for injuries or damage to another person, and Medical Payments (Coverage F) as paying medical bills for people hurt on your property or by your pets (Utah Insurance Department).

How a loss is paid: replacement cost or actual cash value

The NAIC says the dec page usually also describes how damaged property will be paid for. The Utah Insurance Department defines replacement cost as paying to repair or replace with materials of like kind and quality, and actual cash value as paying based on the property's value after depreciation for age and wear (Homeowner's Insurance; glossary). The department also notes that replacement cost is different from your home's market value, which includes the land.

Deductibles

The deductible is the amount you pay out of pocket on each claim before the insurer pays (Utah Insurance Department glossary). The NAIC notes that every dec page states the deductible, sometimes as a dollar amount and sometimes as a percentage, and that how it applies to different coverages is a question for your agent or insurer (NAIC).

Endorsements

An endorsement is an amendment that adds, changes, or removes coverage, also called a rider (glossary). The NAIC says added or increased coverage outside the basic policy shows up in this section. One Utah example: the department says sewer backup is not covered under the standard policy and can be added by endorsement (2024 comparison tables, page 6).

Discounts and premium

The premium is the cost for the policy term, generally after discounts. The NAIC suggests checking the list of discounts and asking your agent or insurer if you think one is missing (NAIC).

Lienholder, agent, and insurer contact

The lienholder's address is where proof of coverage is mailed, so the NAIC suggests checking that it matches what your mortgage company has. The last field is the agent or company that wrote the policy (NAIC).

What the dec page usually does not show

Exclusions live in the policy form, not the dec page. The Utah Insurance Department lists earthquake, sewer backup or sump pump failure, ordinance or law, and intentional loss as typical homeowners exclusions (glossary), and names flood, earthquake, earth movement, and mudslide as perils typically not covered (Homeowner's Insurance). If a coverage is not listed on your dec page as a coverage or endorsement, the policy form is where to check whether it is excluded.

Using your dec page at renewal

  1. Put last year's and this year's pages side by side. Compare each limit, each deductible, the endorsements, and the premium.
  2. Measure the premium change. Our calculator does the math in your browser.
  3. Note changes to the home. The department points out that additions, new insulation, or remodeling add value to a home and may increase its replacement cost (10 Things To Know About Buying Home Insurance).
  4. Know the notice rule. Under Utah Code 31A-21-303, renewal at higher rates or on less favorable terms generally takes effect on the renewal date only if notice was sent at least 30 days before the old policy expires, with exceptions such as a rate change for a whole class of policies. Our non-renewal guide covers the related notice rules.
  5. Bring questions to a licensed agent. The department suggests reviewing coverage with your agent every year (Homeowner's Insurance). You can check any agent's Utah license with the licensee search.

For context on prices, the department's 2025 comparison tables list sample premiums from large Utah insurers for one hypothetical home (10 years old, $450,000 dwelling limit, and a $1,000 deductible for most insurers listed) in four ZIP codes. The department describes them as a general baseline, not a quote.
