What is Utah's new high-risk WUI boundary?

The state wildfire map insurers must use, explained from the statutes and FFSL's own documents.

By the CoverageCheckin editorial team. Published October 26, 2026. Sources checked October 26, 2026. See our editorial policy.

It is a state map, drawn by the Utah Division of Forestry, Fire, and State Lands (FFSL), that marks property at high wildfire risk where homes meet wildland. Utah law uses that boundary to decide which property counts as "high risk wildland urban interface property" for insurance (Utah Code 31A-22-1310) and for a state wildfire fee that counties are set to begin assessing on January 1, 2027 (HB 41, 2026 General Session). What the law says it is

Utah defines the wildland urban interface in general as "the zone where structures and other human development meets, or intermingles with, undeveloped wildland" (Utah Code 65A-1-1). The high-risk boundary is narrower. "High risk wildland urban interface property" means property inside the boundary designated by the state's wildfire risk assessment tool and defined by FFSL rule (Utah Code 65A-8-401). The law directs FFSL to maintain that tool as an online, publicly accessible map, and to set the boundaries by rule using a scientific assessment focused on risk to dwellings (Utah Code 65A-8-203).

How the line is drawn

The state's mapping agency describes the boundary as areas with a Structure Exposure Score (SES) of 7 or higher that also have at least two structures within a 250 meter (820 foot) radius. Inputs include satellite imagery, slope and aspect, FFSL vegetation and fuel maps, weather, fire occurrence, ember loading, housing density, and structure buffers (UGRC, Utah High Risk Wildland Urban Interface). FFSL says the SES 7 and density result is then refined by local experts (FFSL, WUI Zone vs. WUI Boundary).

A proposed rule amendment FFSL published in the August 15, 2026 Utah State Bulletin adds detail: the score uses recent fuel data, the previous ten years of wildfire history, county structure locations, and local weather data, and the map is to be updated once a year, with each county getting a copy at least 60 days before an updated version is adopted (Utah State Bulletin, Aug 15, 2026, R652-126).

What it means for homeowners insurance

  • Insurers that cover wildland urban interface property must use FFSL's boundary to decide whether a property is high risk (Utah Code 31A-22-1310; FFSL).
  • The same law sets notice and disclosure duties tied to wildfire risk, which our non-renewal notice guide and wildfire risk guide cover in more detail.
  • Beginning January 1, 2028, FFSL must keep a database insurers can use to learn a property's triage classification (Utah Code 65A-8-402).

The triage scale and the fee

Inside the boundary, a wildland urban interface coordinator evaluates property on a three-level triage scale and tells the owner the classification, the fee, and resources that could help move the property to a lower-risk level (65A-8-401; 65A-8-402). FFSL sets the fee each year based on a structure's square footage and may tier it by triage level; counties collect it (65A-8-402). HB 41 moved the start of county fee collection from January 1, 2026 to January 1, 2027 (HB 41).

FFSL's public page says an owner can opt out of the assessment but will then be charged the highest fee and considered highest risk, that an owner may instead hire a private assessor certified to FFSL standards, that a fee appeals process will be defined in rule, and that fees go to the Utah Wildfire Fund. It describes a flat square footage fee at first and a fee based on the triage assessment plus square footage from 2028. As of October 26, 2026, the page does not list fee amounts (FFSL, community preparedness).

The same proposed rule amendment describes the three levels: Classification I (high risk) for structures meeting both the defensible space and ignition-resistant construction requirements of the Utah WUI Code, Classification II (very high) for meeting one of the two, and Classification III (extreme) for meeting neither. Property not assessed by January 1, 2028 is assigned Classification III until it is assessed (Utah State Bulletin, Aug 15, 2026).

How the boundary can change

The statute gives counties the formal role. FFSL had to give each county a copy and explanation of the existing tool by August 6, 2026. For any expansion, FFSL must give counties 60 days' notice; a county then has 30 days to send written recommended changes, and FFSL has 30 days to adopt them or explain in writing why it is modifying or rejecting them (Utah Code 65A-8-203). FFSL says owners can join a lot assessment waitlist once the program goes live (FFSL).
