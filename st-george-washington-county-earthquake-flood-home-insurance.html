St. George homes: Hurricane Fault, flash floods, and what standard policies exclude

Hazard facts from the Utah Geological Survey, USGS, and the state flood program, plus questions to ask an agent.

By the CoverageCheckin editorial team. Published October 26, 2026. Sources checked October 26, 2026. See our editorial policy.

St. George and the rest of Washington County sit near one of Utah's most active faults and along rivers that have destroyed homes within the last few decades. Both hazards fall into categories the Utah Insurance Department lists as typically not covered by a homeowners policy: flood, earthquake, earth movement, and mudslide (Utah Insurance Department, homeowners insurance). If you want time to ask about them before your policy renews, our free renewal check can send you a reminder ahead of that date.

For how earthquake coverage is usually offered in Utah, see does homeowners insurance cover earthquakes in Utah? To see what your current policy already lists, see how to read your declarations page. If your concern is wildfire on the edge of town, see what Utah's high-risk WUI boundary is.

The Hurricane fault

The Utah Geological Survey names the Hurricane fault in southern Utah among the most active faults in the state, alongside the Wasatch fault (UGS, Utah Faults). In its summary of the St. George and Hurricane geologic-hazard maps, the survey says large damaging earthquakes are rare in southwestern Utah but that active faults in the area can produce earthquakes as large as about magnitude 7 (UGS, Geologic-Hazard Maps for St. George-Hurricane Area).

The area's most recent damaging example is the magnitude (ML) 5.8 earthquake of September 2, 1992, centered about 5 miles east-southeast of St. George. The UGS report on it says it was the largest earthquake in the St. George area since 1902, that its data suggest but do not prove it came from buried slip on the Hurricane fault, and that no surface faulting was found. Ground shaking damaged buildings in Hurricane, La Verkin, Washington, St. George, and other communities. The earthquake also triggered the Springdale landslide, which destroyed three homes in the Balanced Rock Hills subdivision (UGS Circular 88).

That last detail is a useful question to bring to an agent: a landslide started by an earthquake touches two exclusions at once (earthquake and earth movement). How a specific policy or endorsement handles that depends on its wording.

Flooding is the most widespread hazard

The UGS says that, on an annual basis, flooding is the most widespread and damaging geologic hazard in southwestern Utah (UGS). Two recent floods show why:

  • January 2005, Santa Clara River. The river at St. George peaked at 6,200 cubic feet per second on January 11, 2005, the highest flow in the gauge's record, with an estimated recurrence interval of 25 to 50 years. Several houses collapsed into the river as it shifted course. The floods along the Santa Clara River destroyed or damaged 28 homes, with an estimated $85 million in personal property damage and another $140 million in damage to local infrastructure, about $225 million combined (USGS Fact Sheet 2006-3085). The National Weather Service notes the homes had been built on river sediments left by earlier floods, which the 2005 flows eroded (NWS, Utah flood safety).
  • 2010, Virgin River. During a four-day rain event, flows on the Virgin River near St. George rose from 100 to 25,000 cubic feet per second in a 24 hour period (NWS).

A 25 to 50 year recurrence interval works out to roughly a 2% to 4% chance of a flow that large in any given year (1 in 50 and 1 in 25). The state's flood program defines a high-risk flood zone as an area with a 1% or higher chance of flooding each year, and reminds owners in lower-risk zones that low risk does not equal no risk (Utah Flood Hazards, flood insurance).

Flood is a separate policy

Most homeowners and renters policies do not cover flooding (Utah Flood Hazards). The Utah Insurance Department says flood coverage requires a separate policy and usually has a 30 day waiting period before it takes effect (Utah Insurance Department, 2024 comparison tables). According to the state's flood program:

  • National Flood Insurance Program policies for a home cover up to $250,000 for the building and $100,000 for contents. Private flood insurance is another option.
  • A lender can require flood insurance even if a home is outside a FEMA high-risk zone.
  • No Utah statute or regulation requires a seller to disclose flood information on a specified form, so buyers need to research a home's flood risk themselves.
  • Federal disaster aid requires a presidential declaration, and most Utah floods do not receive one.
  • You can look up an address on FEMA's Map Service Center, linked from the state page.

The federal program's consumer site is FloodSmart.gov.

Expansive soil and other ground hazards

The UGS says expansive soil and rock is the most troublesome construction condition in the St. George and Hurricane area, and that many buildings there have cracked foundations and walls as a result. Its 14 hazard maps cover a 366 square mile study area (UGS). The survey's Utah Geologic Hazards Portal lets you view mapped hazards near an address. Earth movement is one of the perils the Insurance Department lists as typically not covered (Utah Insurance Department); whether a particular soil problem falls under that wording is a question for your agent or insurer.

What the state's sample premiums show for Hurricane (84737)

The Insurance Department's 2025 comparison tables list sample HO3 premiums from Utah insurers for one hypothetical home: 10 years old, protection class 1 to 6, $450,000 dwelling limit, $300,000 liability, and a $1,000 deductible for most insurers listed. Washington County's sample ZIP is Hurricane, 84737. The department calls the tables a general baseline, not a quote, and the sample ZIPs change from year to year. Questions to bring to an agent

  • Does my policy include an earthquake endorsement, and what deductible applies to it?
  • How does my policy word the earth movement exclusion?
  • Is my address in a mapped flood zone, and does my lender require flood insurance?
  • If I want flood coverage, when would it take effect?
