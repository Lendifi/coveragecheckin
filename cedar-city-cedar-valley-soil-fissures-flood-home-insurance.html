Cedar City and Cedar Valley homes: collapsible soil, earth fissures, and flood

Facts from the Utah Geological Survey and the Census Bureau, plus questions to ask an agent.

By the CoverageCheckin editorial team. Published October 28, 2026. Sources checked October 28, 2026. See our editorial policy.

In Cedar Valley, the hazards that cost the most are on the ground, not in the trees: the Utah Geological Survey names collapsible soil and flooding as the costliest geologic hazards there, and ground cracks have already damaged a new subdivision in Enoch. Flood and earth movement are perils the Utah Insurance Department lists as typically not covered by a homeowners policy, along with earthquake and mudslide (Utah Insurance Department, homeowners insurance). What the 2025 UGS hazard mapping found

A 2025 UGS study mapped geologic hazards across the Cedar City, Cedar City NW, Enoch, and Kanarraville quadrangles, which cover most of Cedar Valley (UGS Special Study 177). It says:

  • Cedar Valley has seen substantial population growth since the 1990s, and growth is expanding into areas less suited for development.
  • Collapsible soil (soil prone to collapse when it gets wet) and flooding have been the costliest geologic hazards affecting the valley.
  • Earth fissures tied to groundwater decline have affected new development in Enoch and will be a growing concern until aquifer levels stabilize.
  • Other mapped hazards include landslide, rockfall, expansive soil and rock, shallow groundwater, liquefaction, and radon.

The mapping is viewable on the UGS Utah Geologic Hazards Portal. The UGS notes its maps are for general planning and are not a substitute for a site-specific investigation.

Subsidence and the Enoch fissure

A UGS investigation found that the ground surface across more than 110 square miles of Cedar Valley has subsided as much as 4 feet since 1950, and that groundwater pumping in excess of recharge has lowered the groundwater surface by as much as 114 feet since 1939 (UGS Survey Notes, land subsidence and earth fissures in Cedar Valley). The survey found at least 3.9 miles of earth fissures in the valley. The longest, a 2.4-mile fissure in north Enoch found in 2009, damaged streets, sidewalks, and curbs in a new subdivision and reversed the flow of a sewer line. The UGS concluded that continued groundwater overdraft will likely lengthen existing fissures and form new ones.

Earth movement is on the Insurance Department's list of perils typically not covered (Utah Insurance Department). Whether settling, collapsing soil, or a ground crack falls under that wording on a particular policy is a question for your agent or insurer.

Newer homes, same ground

Cedar City's housing is young. Census Bureau estimates (2020 to 2024) put the median year built at 1999, and 6,407 of about 13,572 housing units (47.2%) were built in 2000 or later (ACS 2024 5-year, table B25034, via Census Reporter). We computed the share from those counts. A newer house can still sit on the soils the UGS mapped, which is why its hazard maps point builders to site-specific investigations.

Questions to bring to an agent

  • How does my policy word the earth movement exclusion, and would soil settling or collapse after water gets into the ground be treated differently from a burst pipe?
  • Is my address in a mapped flood zone, and does my lender require flood insurance?
  • If I want flood coverage, when would it take effect?
  • Does my policy include an earthquake endorsement, and what deductible applies to it?
