Does homeowners insurance cover hail damage to a roof in Utah?

Hail coverage, roof deductibles, and Utah claim rules, from the NAIC, the Utah Insurance Department, and state hazard data.

By the CoverageCheckin editorial team. Published November 16, 2026. Sources checked November 16, 2026. See our editorial policy.

Hail storms in Utah are usually small and short, but a single storm can damage roofs across a neighborhood. This page explains what public sources say about hail coverage in a typical homeowners policy, how roof claims are often paid, and what Utah law says about claims and renewals. To see your deductible and roof terms before a storm, check your declarations page and set up a free renewal check so you get a reminder to review your policy before it renews.

Is hail usually covered?

The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) says homeowners policies in most states cover damage caused by windstorm and hail, while policies in coastal areas often exclude it (NAIC, A Consumer's Guide to Home Insurance). The NAIC also notes that some policies exclude wind and hail, so the only way to know is to read your own policy or ask your insurer (NAIC, Rebuilding After a Storm).

The NAIC guide adds that a homeowners policy is not a maintenance contract: it insures against perils such as fire, wind, and hail, and does not pay to repair items that simply wear out (NAIC consumer guide). For an older roof, that difference between storm damage and wear is often part of the claim discussion.

How often does hail hit Utah?

The State of Utah Hazard Mitigation Plan, using the federal NCEI storm database, counts 563 hail events on 306 days in Utah from 1955 to 2022, with more than $6.5 million in recorded property damage (Utah Hazard Mitigation Plan, Severe Weather chapter). The plan says hail is more prevalent in the northern part of the state, and that FEMA's National Risk Index rates hail risk generally higher in the populated Wasatch Front counties.

The largest recorded events in the plan's table were on October 5, 1994, in Salt Lake County: $5 million in estimated property damage listed for Cottonwood Heights and $500,000 for Salt Lake City. The table also lists $300,000 in American Fork on August 26, 1998. The plan cautions that of 59 hail events recorded from May 2017 to August 2022, only one had reported damages, which it says is likely not inclusive of insured losses. In other words, the public database undercounts what homeowners actually claim.

Wind and hail deductibles

The NAIC says some policies have a separate deductible for wind or hail, and some have a special deductible for a specific part of the home, like the roof. It can be a flat dollar amount or a percentage (NAIC, Post-Disaster Claims Guide). A percentage deductible is figured on the insured value of the home, not on the size of the claim. Using the NAIC's own example, a 2% deductible on a home insured for $250,000 is $5,000. Your declarations page shows which deductibles apply; our guide to reading a declarations page walks through where to find them.

Replacement cost vs actual cash value on roofs

The NAIC explains that even a replacement cost policy may limit what it pays for certain surfaces such as roofs, and in some cases a policy pays actual cash value on the roof but replacement cost on the rest of the home (NAIC, Post-Disaster Claims Guide). In the NAIC's example, two neighbors each have $15,000 of roof damage and a $1,000 deductible. The replacement cost policyholder receives $14,000. The actual cash value policyholder, after $10,000 of depreciation, receives $4,000 (NAIC, Rebuilding After a Storm). The Utah Insurance Department gives the same definitions of the two terms (Utah Insurance Department, Homeowner's Insurance).

What the Utah Insurance Department says to do after damage

  • Contact your insurer or agent as soon as possible and ask what documents and forms you will need.
  • Take photos or video of the damage.
  • Make repairs needed to prevent further damage, such as covering a leaking roof, but do not have permanent repairs made until the insurer has inspected and you have agreed on the cost.
  • Save all receipts, including for temporary repairs.

That list comes from the Department's home claims information page, which also says interior water damage from a storm, when there is no damage to the roof or walls, is not covered under most standard policies.

Claim deadlines in Utah law

Under Utah Code 31A-21-312, missing a notice or proof-of-loss deadline in the policy does not invalidate a claim if it was not reasonably possible to meet it and notice was given as soon as reasonably possible; the insurer must also furnish proof-of-loss forms on request. Under Utah Code 31A-21-313, a lawsuit on a first-party policy generally must begin within three years after the inception of the loss. Your policy may have its own reporting terms, so read them or ask your agent.

Will a hail claim cost me my policy?

Utah law limits this. Under Utah Code 31A-21-303 (Subsection 5(b)), an insurer may not non-renew a homeowners policy solely because of one claim in a 36-month period for damage that came only from wind, hail, or lightning and was not preventable with reasonable care. Our article on whether a Utah insurer can non-renew you after a claim covers the details, and why your renewal went up covers pricing.

Questions to bring to your insurer or an agent

  • Does my policy cover wind and hail, and is there a separate wind/hail or roof deductible?
  • Is that deductible a flat amount or a percentage of my dwelling limit, and what is it in dollars?
  • Is my roof settled at replacement cost or actual cash value?
  • What are the reporting deadlines for a claim?
